Study Abroad Guide: how to get a funded master's or PhD

When I decided to do my master's abroad, the hard part wasn't getting accepted. It was knowing where to start. The information was spread across Facebook groups, years-old posts, a friend who had already left, university websites and comments buried under YouTube videos.

This guide puts it in one place, in the order you will actually need it: from opening your laptop to the admission letter. It covers what worked for me, and what didn't.

By Ahmed Mahgoub. B.Sc. Ain Shams University, now M.S. at KAIST, South Korea.

Last checked October 2026. Fees, test rules and deadlines change every year, so always confirm on the official website.

Step 0

Why would anyone pay you to study?

Before you look for a professor, understand what you are actually offering. Once this clicks, every email you write gets better.

In short: look for a research degree, not a professional one. In research degrees you work in a lab on funded projects, so you get paid. You are not asking for a favor. You are offering something the professor needs.

Which degree are you looking for?

Research M.S.

About 2 years

Some courses, but the core is your work in the lab.

Ph.D.

4 to 5 years

You are a full-time researcher.

Integrated M.S.–Ph.D.

Straight through

From your bachelor's directly into a PhD track.

There is also the professional (taught) master's: you pay tuition, take courses and usually have no lab. The research master's is different. You work, so it is usually funded. That's the one this guide is about.

Who pays? Four sources

The professor

Research assistantship

Paid from the professor's project funding. The most common route.

Government scholarship

For example GKS in Korea

Secure, but competitive with fixed deadlines.

University scholarship

Tuition waivers, stipends

Often comes together with the professor's funding.

Self-funded

Your own money

Possible, but rarely needed for a research degree if you search well.

What your life in the lab looks like

A lab works like a small company. The professor is the manager who brings in projects and money. Under them are postdocs, PhD students and master's students like you. You get a desk, a computer, colleagues from different countries and a monthly stipend. You work on part of a real project paid for by a company or a government: simulation, design or experiments. Every week there is a lab meeting where you show your progress, and in the end your work becomes a paper.

So you are not a student being paid for. You are a researcher being paid for work.

Why the university cares

Look at how the QS World University Rankings score universities. The share of international students counts for 5%. Citations per faculty, which is the research that people like you produce, counts for 20%. Universities don't bring you in to fill a quota. They bring you in because you run the research.

The professor finishes the project, the university publishes and climbs the rankings, the country gains skilled people, and you get a degree, experience and a salary. It's an exchange, not charity. When you email a professor, you are offering something they need.

Step 1: find a professor
Step 1

Find universities, professors and labs

You are not applying to a country or even a university first. You are looking for a person: a professor whose work matches yours.

In short:
  • Use the QS ranking for your subject, and look at the top 100, not just the top 10.
  • Weigh time difference, language and how open the country is to foreigners.
  • Go from the department's faculty page, to the professor's page, to the lab website.
  • Track everyone in one spreadsheet.

Start with the ranking for your subject

The overall QS ranking is a start, but the QS ranking by subject (for example Mechanical, Aeronautical and Manufacturing Engineering) is more useful. It shows you where your field is strong, which is not always the same list as the famous names.

It's not only about the rank

Think aboutWhy it matters
Time differenceYou will be emailing and calling the professor, and calling your family. From Cairo, Korea and Japan are about 7 hours ahead, Germany 1 hour behind, Canada's east coast 7 hours behind and the US west coast about 10 hours behind.
LanguageIs the program and daily life in English, or will you need the local language?
OpennessHow welcoming the country is to foreigners, and what everyday life is like there.

From the faculty page to the lab

  1. Search the university and department, and open the faculty page.
  2. Filter by your research area.
  3. Open each professor's page: research area, recent work, contact email.
  4. Open the lab website and check four things: what they research, whether they say they are taking students, whether there are international students in the lab, and which projects are running now.

Keep one sheet for everyone

You will contact dozens of people. Keep a single spreadsheet with: university, professor, research area, how well it matches you, whether the lab has international students, the date you emailed, the date you followed up, and the reply.

My mistakes, so you don't repeat them

Don't stop at the top 10

The top 10 is crowded. Go down to the top 100 and you'll find far more chances.

Follow up

If the work is very close to yours, send again after one or two weeks.

Send in their working hours

4 pm in Cairo is 9 am in Toronto. Check their time before you hit send.

Step 2: the first email
Step 2

The first email

Professors get a lot of these. Yours needs to be easy to open, easy to read and clearly written for them.

In short: one link instead of a pile of attachments, three parts in the body (their research, you, one sentence that connects the two), and a lot of emails. Expect roughly one reply for every 10 to 20 you send.

How I sent my work, and what changed

1. Everything as PDFs

Up to 15 attachments in one email. Nobody has time to open all of that.

2. A Drive link

A folder of files. Still nobody opens it.

3. A personal website

One link with everything. It doesn't need to be fancy, a simple page is enough.

What goes in the email

  1. Their research. Show you read it. Mention a specific paper or project.
  2. You. Your degree, your most relevant project, and the link to your work.
  3. One sentence that connects you. Why your background fits their work.

Targeting or volume?

TargetingVolume
WhoA few chosen professorsEvery professor in your field
Each emailWritten fully for that personLess time per email
ChancesFewerMore

I went with volume: more than 800 emails, close to a thousand. But volume can't mean copy and paste. Twenty emails that all start "I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to express my keen interest in…" read as one email sent twenty times, and professors notice.

About AI: use it to polish your language and to summarize a professor's papers so you understand them faster. Don't let it write the email for you. It shows.

From my supervisor

"I read your email by chance." So send a lot. You never know which one gets read.

Step 3: the reply and interview
Step 3

When they reply: the interview

A positive reply is not an admission. It's the first step, and how you handle it matters.

The reply is usually one of three

"No position"

Still a reply. Thank them and ask if they can suggest a colleague.

"Send your CV"

They're still deciding. Send it the same day.

"Let's meet"

This is what you want. Agree on a time quickly.

Sometimes there's a filter first

Some professors test you before they meet you. These are real replies I received:

Before the call

  1. Read their last two or three papers.
  2. Prepare a two-minute summary of your work: who you are, what you did, what you want.
  3. Test your camera, microphone and internet.
  4. Work out the time difference, then check it again.

The interview itself

Usually on Zoom or Teams, from 15 minutes to an hour. Expect questions like:

  • Tell me about yourself.
  • Why this lab?
  • What did you do in your last project?
  • Why do you want this degree?

Answer with real examples. If you don't know something, say so. Never make up an answer. They are looking for someone who can learn, not someone who knows everything.

You ask questions too

At the end they will ask "Do you have any questions?" Don't say no.

Funding

Is it guaranteed, and for how many years?

Project

What would I work on?

Lab

Who is in the lab now?

Next step

What happens after this call?

And if you can, talk to a current student in the lab.

After the call

  1. A thank-you email the same day. Two lines is enough.
  2. A polite follow-up if two weeks pass with no reply. It's not rude.
  3. Apply. If they tell you to apply, the next stage starts.

The interview isn't an exam. It's a conversation between two people who each need something from the other.

Step 4: the application
Step 4

The application

The professor saying yes doesn't admit you. The university's admissions committee does. That's what the application is for.

Professor agreesUniversity applicationCommittee decidesOfficial admission

Read the website first, then ask

The usual habit is to ask someone who went before you. Their information may be out of date, and every case is different. The admissions website is the reference: it's updated every year and everything is written there. Read it first, then ask what's still unclear.

The requirements sections are similar everywhere

Citizenship

Who is allowed to apply.

Academic qualification

Bachelor's or master's degree.

English

TOEFL, IELTS or similar, with a minimum score.

GPA

Sometimes a minimum.

Documents

CV, transcripts, recommendation letters.

Deadlines

And there are no exceptions.

Make a checklist for each university and fill it from its own website. Things to watch: some universities exclude certain nationalities, and your English score usually has to be valid up to the deadline.

Step 5: documents
Step 5

Documents

Every university has its own details, but the parts look alike. The examples here are from the KAIST application guide.

The professor's name goes in the form

This is where the professor you talked to makes a difference. At KAIST, master's applicants can list up to three faculty members. PhD applicants list one, and contacting them before applying is strongly recommended.

It's all online, in steps

You fill the application step by step and pay an application fee at the end (USD 80 at KAIST when I applied). After you pay, you can't edit anything, so review everything first.

The document list

Read the required documents table line by line. There are things you won't expect. KAIST, for example, asked for proof of both parents' citizenship.

Statement of purpose and short questions

KAIST asked for a statement of purpose of up to 5,000 bytes (roughly characters), plus short answers to questions such as what you studied, why this major and your plans after graduating. Structure it the same way as your email: who you are, what you did, what you want.

CV

KAIST had no required format. Other universities do, so check.

Recommendation letters

The university emails your recommenders directly and they upload the letter, so you won't see it. Pick people who actually know your work and care about you, not just the biggest title. Ask early. This is the most awkward part of the file, and it takes time.

Degree certificate and transcript

Both need the grading system explained, and certification (apostille or embassy) is recommended.

If you're applying from Egypt

Egypt is not part of the Apostille Convention, so certification goes through several offices in order:

Your university (certified copy)Supreme Council of UniversitiesMinistry of Foreign AffairsEmbassy of the country you're going to

Start early, and certify copies rather than originals, because many universities don't return documents.

Step 6: English tests
Step 6

English tests

Most programs accept several tests, each with its own minimum. The score has to arrive before the deadline.

Which test?

TOEIC

The easiest

Listening and reading only, multiple choice. Mainly accepted in Korea and Japan.

IELTS Academic

The broadest

All four skills, including speaking and writing. Accepted almost everywhere.

TOEFL iBT

Accepted everywhere

All four skills, at a test center or from home. New score scale from January 2026.

Only applying to Korea? TOEIC is enough. More than one country? Take IELTS or TOEFL.

How to register

  • TOEIC: through the local ETS partner in your country. Choose "Listening and Reading". It's two hours and 200 questions.
  • IELTS: through the British Council or IDP. Choose Academic, not General Training.
  • TOEFL: directly on the ETS website, at a center or with the Home Edition.

What happened to me

I prepared for the TOEIC on paper, because an AI tool told me there were two versions. At the test center there was only the computer version, and it's a different experience. I ended up retaking the whole test.

Confirm the format on the official website, not with AI, and practice on a computer the way the real test runs. Sites like estudyme.com have full online TOEIC practice tests.

If you're applying from Egypt

  • TOEIC is run by Amideast: 38 Mohie El Din Abou El Ezz St., Dokki, Cairo, with a branch in Alexandria. Hotline 19263. You can book online or pay at a branch, and there's a free practice test on their website.
  • IELTS through the British Council or IDP, with centers in Cairo, Giza and Alexandria. Around EGP 8,000 to 10,000. Check the current price before you book.
  • TOEFL iBT was about USD 175 on the ETS price list for Egypt.
Step 7: after you submit
Step 7

After you submit: the result

The result comes on the application portal. If you did the steps before this one well, funding is usually part of the offer.

In the same letter, KAIST noted that over the last five years about 94% of international students received a tuition waiver and 91% received the basic stipend. The funding from Step 0 is normal here, not the exception.

The funding is in the letter

At KAIST, the admission certificate states your financial support: a tuition scholarship for the length of the program and a stipend, with part of it guaranteed together with national health insurance, and the rest depending on research projects. That's the professor funding from Step 0, now in writing.

You have to respond

Accept

Send the documents listed in the registration guide. That's what confirms you're coming.

Decline

Officially, through the portal, before the deadline.

Shipping your documents

The certified originals go by express courier. Budget for this from the start.

If you're applying from Egypt

I sent mine with DHL Express Worldwide. It arrived in four days and cost about EGP 3,850. Keep the tracking number and follow it until it arrives.
